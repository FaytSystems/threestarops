Restaurant par levels and prep sheets

Count with confidence. Prep with a plan.

A useful par sheet turns “we might need more” into a clear quantity to review. Start with consistent units, a real count, and a target your kitchen understands.

Give every item a clear counting rule

Choose a name, unit, location, and target for each item. “Tomatoes” is a start; “tomatoes, pounds, walk-in, target 24” gives the person counting something concrete. If purchasing uses cases and counting uses pounds, record the pack conversion so the order quantity does not depend on memory.

Keep the counting route consistent. A sheet arranged in the order of your walk-in, dry storage, or station is easier to follow than a list that sends someone back and forth. ThreeStarOps lets you build ingredients around vendors, units, locations, and stations.

Use a target, then account for what is already coming

For a basic replenishment check, start with the target quantity, subtract usable stock on hand, and subtract confirmed incoming stock that will arrive before it is needed. If the result is below zero, the immediate top-up is zero. This is a planning starting point; review demand, lead times, pack sizes, and your actual service needs before placing an order.

Illustrative example

Your target is 24 units. You count 9 usable units, and 6 more are confirmed to arrive in time. The starting top-up is 24 − 9 − 6 = 9 units.

Separate what to order from what to prep

An ingredient shortage and a prep shortage are different jobs. You might have enough whole ingredients but too little prepared product for the next service. Give prep records their own unit, batch size, station, and completion check.

A practical prep sheet identifies the item, the usable amount already ready, the amount to make, the assigned station or person, and who reviews completion. That makes the sheet useful at the start of the shift and during handover, rather than only when it was written.

Review the target when the business changes

A target should reflect how your kitchen operates. Revisit it when the menu, delivery schedule, pack size, or service volume changes. Keep notes about repeated shortages and unused excess so a chef can adjust the target with context.

Start with a manageable group of items instead of rebuilding every list at once. Run the same counting and review routine, correct the units, and then expand it. Consistency is more useful than a long sheet nobody trusts.
